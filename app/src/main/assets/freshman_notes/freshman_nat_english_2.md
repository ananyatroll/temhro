## Chapter 1: Active and Passive Voice

Voice refers to the form of a verb that indicates whether the grammatical subject performs the action or is the receiver of the action. Most English verbs have two voices: the active voice and the passive voice.

- When the verb is in **active voice**, the subject of the sentence performs the action (the subject does the action).
- When the verb is in **passive voice**, the subject of the sentence is the receiver of the action (the subject receives the action).

### Rules of Changing Active to Passive

1. The sentence must have objects (contain a transitive verb).
   - **Transitive verbs** require an object to give complete meaning. A sentence can be changed into passive voice only if it has both subject and object.
   - **Intransitive verbs** do not require an object and cannot be changed into passive voice. Examples: appear, arrive, come, become, go, happen, occur, rain, stay, walk, run, resemble.
   - Example: "The baby is crying." â†’ No object, so no passive is possible.

2. The object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive sentence.

3. The "Be" form must be used according to the tense of the verb (be, am, is, are, was, were, being, been) followed by the past participle form of the main verb.

4. Past participle (V3) form of the main verb must be used in all tenses of passive voice.

5. Preposition "by" can be added when the agent is important.
   - Do not include "by" when:
     - The doer is unknown: "The money was stolen."
     - The doer is people in general: "Black cats are believed to bring bad luck."
     - The doer is unimportant: "This bridge was built in 1889."

### Passive Voice by Tense

**Simple Present**
- Active: S + V1 + s/es + O
- Passive: S + am/is/are + V3 + by + O
- Example: The caretaker locks the door. â†’ The door is locked by the caretaker.

**Present Continuous**
- Active: S + am/is/are + V-ing + O
- Passive: S + am/is/are + being + V3 + by + O
- Example: The singer is singing a song beautifully. â†’ The song is being sung beautifully by the singer.

**Present Perfect**
- Active: S + has/have + V3 + O
- Passive: S + has/have + been + V3 + by + O
- Example: Ali has scored two goals. â†’ Two goals have been scored by Ali.

**Simple Past**
- Active: S + V2 + O
- Passive: S + was/were + V3 + by + O
- Example: He took these photos. â†’ These photos were taken by him.

**Past Continuous**
- Active: S + was/were + V-ing + O
- Passive: S + was/were + being + V3 + by + O
- Example: She was reading a book. â†’ The book was being read by her.

**Past Perfect**
- Active: S + had + V3 + O
- Passive: S + had + been + V3 + by + O
- Example: Ruth had given a gift to Abiy. â†’ A gift had been given to Abiy by Ruth.

**Simple Future**
- Active: S + will + V1 + O
- Passive: S + will + be + V3 + by + O
- Example: We will celebrate her birthday. â†’ Her birthday will be celebrated by us.

**Future Perfect**
- Active: S + will + have + V3 + O
- Passive: S + will + have + been + V3 + by + O
- Example: John will have repaired his bicycle. â†’ His bicycle will have been repaired by John.

**Imperatives**
- Active: V1 + O
- Passive: Let + O + be + V3
- Example: Shut the door. â†’ Let the door be shut.
- Example: Give up smoking. â†’ Let smoking be given up.

**Infinitives**
- Active: S + verb + O + to V
- Passive: S + verb + O + to be + V3
- Example: I want to write the letter. â†’ I want the letter to be written.

**Present Modals**
- Passive: modal + be + V3
- Example: He could repair the car. â†’ The car could be repaired in two days.
- Example: Students should study the lessons. â†’ The lessons should be studied by students.

**Perfect Modals**
- Passive: modal + have + been + V3
- Example: He could have repaired the car. â†’ The car could have been repaired in two days.
- Example: You should have studied the book. â†’ The book should have been studied by you.

### When to Use Passive Voice

- When the agent is not known: "The door is broken."
- When we don't want to mention the agent.
- When we want to emphasize the object rather than the subject: "The president was killed in 1998."
- When we want to emphasize the agent: "The new library will be opened by the Queen."

### Double Object Verbs

When a sentence has two objects (give, tell, send, show, bring, write, pay, offer, etc.), either object can become the subject:
- Active: Meron gave me this book.
- Passive 1: The book was given to me by Meron.
- Passive 2: I was given this book by Meron.

### Interrogative Passive Rules

| Active Voice | Passive Voice |
|---|---|
| Do/does + subject + verb + object? | Am/is/are + subject + V3 + by + agent? |
| Am/is/are + subject + verb + ing + object? | Am/is/are + subject + being + V3 + by + agent? |
| Did + subject + verb + object? | Was/were + subject + V3 + by + agent? |
| Was/were + subject + verb + ing + object? | Was/were + subject + being + V3 + by + agent? |
| Have/has/had + subject + V3 + object? | Have/has/had + subject + been + V3 + by + agent? |
| Modal + subject + verb + object? | Modal + subject + be + V3 + by + agent? |
| Wh word + auxiliary + subject + verb + object? | Wh word + auxiliary + subject + V3 + by + agent? |

### Examples of Interrogative Passives

- Does she cook the food? â†’ Is the food cooked by her?
- Is she cooking the food? â†’ Is the food being cooked by her?
- Did she cook the food? â†’ Was the food cooked by her?
- Has she cooked the food? â†’ Has the food been cooked by her?
- Can she cook the food? â†’ Can the food be cooked by her?
- Why did she punish you? â†’ Why were you punished by her?
- Who wrote this letter? â†’ By whom was this letter written?
- What did you eat? â†’ What was eaten by you?

## Chapter 2: Modals of Suggestion and Advice

### Should, Ought to, Had Better

These three verbs are modal verbs used to express ideas like ability, advice, and obligation. They are always followed by the simple form of a verb.

- **Should** is the most common way to give advice.
- **Ought to** expresses obligation.
- **Had better** expresses urgency, demand, threat, or worry â€” it includes the idea that something bad will happen if you do not follow the advice.

### Affirmative, Negative, and Question Forms

| Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|
| I failed my test. You should study harder. | Young children shouldn't watch violent TV shows. | I have a problem. Should I call my parents or my friend? |
| It's really cold outside. You ought to wear a warm jacket. | You ought not to watch TV before finishing your homework. | (Rarely used in question form) |
| You had better slow down. You are driving too fast! | You had better not forget to pay your tuition. | (Not usually used in question form) |

### Negative Contractions

| Negative Sentence | Contraction |
|---|---|
| You should not put hot water on a burn. | You shouldn't put hot water on a burn. |
| You ought not to put hot water on a burn. | (No contraction) |
| You had better not put hot water on a burn. | (No contraction) |

### Sentence Patterns

- Subject + modal + basic verb: "You should study harder."
- Wrong: "You should to study harder."
- Wrong: "You had better slowing down."
- Wrong: "She ought to to drink more water."
- WH-questions: "What should I do about my problem?" / "Where should we have dinner tonight?"

### Usage Summary

**Should** â€” my opinion / giving advice
- You should finish your homework before you go out.
- I should read the script on the train tomorrow.

**Ought to** â€” obligation
- You ought to finish your homework before you go out. (it is your obligation)

**Had better** â€” urgency, demand, threat, worry
- There's someone moving about downstairs. We had better call the police, quickly.
- He had better not be late again or he'll be in trouble.

## Chapter 4: Direct and Reported Speech

### What is Reported Speech?

When someone speaks directly (direct speech), and you tell somebody what they said, that is reported speech.

Example:
- Direct: "My parents are very well."
- Reported: Lencho said that his parents are very well.

### Tense Changes in Reported Speech

| Direct Speech | Reported Speech |
|---|---|
| Simple Present: "I am an investor." | Simple Past: He said he was an investor. |
| Present Continuous: "I'm having lunch with Hunde." | Past Continuous: He said he was having lunch with Hunde. |
| Present Perfect: "I have been to Israel three times." | Past Perfect: He said he had been to Israel three times. |
| Present Perfect Continuous: "I have been studying English." | Past Perfect Continuous: He said he had been studying English. |
| Simple Past: "I bought a new shirt." | Past Perfect: He said he had bought a new shirt. |
| Past Continuous: "It was raining earlier." | Past Perfect Continuous: He said it had been raining earlier. |
| Past Perfect: "The game had started when I arrived." | Past Perfect (no change): He said the game had started when he arrived. |
| Past Perfect Continuous: "I had already been living in Israel for three weeks." | Past Perfect Continuous (no change): He said that he had already been living in Israel for three weeks. |

### Modal Verb Changes

| Modal | Direct Speech | Reported Speech |
|---|---|---|
| will | She said, "I will teach English online tomorrow." | She said she would teach English online tomorrow. |
| can | She said, "I can teach English online." | She said she could teach English online. |
| must | She said, "I must have a computer to teach English online." | She said she had to have a computer to teach English online. |
| shall | She said, "What shall we learn today?" | She asked what we should learn that day. |
| may | She said, "May I open a new folder?" | She asked if she might open a new folder. |

### Modals That Do Not Change

The modal auxiliaries could, might, ought to, should, and would are not changed in reported speech. Must also does not change when it expresses probability.

### Pronoun Changes

| Direct Speech | Reported Speech |
|---|---|
| I | he/she |
| you | I/me/him/her/them |
| he/she | he/she |
| he/she/it | he/she/it |
| we | they |
| us | them |
| our | their |
| they | they |
| them | them |
| their | their |

Example:
- Direct: They said, "We are late."
- Reported: They said they were late.

### Adverb Changes

| Direct Speech | Reported Speech |
|---|---|
| now | then/at that moment |
| today | that day |
| tonight | that night |
| this week/month/year | that week/month/year |
| tomorrow | the next day/the following day |

- Direct: He said, "I went to Finfine last week." â†’ Reported: He said that he had gone to Finfine the week before.
- Direct: Ifabasa said, "I need the books now." â†’ Reported: Ifabasa said that she needed the books then.
- Direct: Sinan said, "The modules are here." â†’ Reported: Sinan said that the modules were there.
- Direct: Tiya said, "This is important." â†’ Reported: Tiya said that was important.

### Reporting Verbs

Said, told, and asked are not the only reporting verbs. Others include: accused, admitted, advised, agreed, apologized, complained, denied, explained, implied, invited, ordered, promised, replied, suggested, alleged, begged, boasted, and thought.

### When Tenses Do NOT Change

If the reporting verb is in the present, perfect, or future tense (says, has said, will say), the reported speech will be retained.

Examples:
- Direct: She says, "I wash my hair every day." â†’ Reported: She says that she washes her hair every day.
- Direct: She has said, "I go to church every Sunday." â†’ Reported: She has said that she goes to church every Sunday.

If the reporting sentence expresses a general truth or fact, the reported sentence is retained.
- Direct: The teacher said, "The sun rises in the east." â†’ Reported: The teacher said that the sun rises in the east.

If the speaker reports something immediately or soon after it was said, the reported clause often remains as spoken.
- Direct: He said, "The next stop is the main gate." â†’ Reported: He said that the next stop is the main gate.

### Reporting Statements

Pattern: Subject + Reporting Verb + (that) + subject + tense change + rest of sentence.

Example:
- Direct: Jalie said, "I will never forget you."
- Reported: Jalie said (to me) that she would never forget me.

The most common reporting verbs for statements are: tell (tell + object pronoun, which is necessary) and say (to + object pronoun, which is optional). Other reporting verbs include add, admit, agree, decide, etc.

### Reporting Questions

**Yes/No Questions:**
Pattern: Subject + reporting verb + if/whether + subject + tense change + rest of sentence.
- Direct: "Is your friend an Ethiopian?" she asked.
- Reported: She asked him if/whether his friend was an Ethiopian.

Reported questions use normal word order and do not have question marks.

**Wh-Questions:**
Pattern: Subject + reporting verb + question word + subject + tense change + rest of sentence.
- Direct: I asked her, "Where does she live?" â†’ Reported: I asked her where she lived.
- Direct: "Where is the registrar?" asked the student. â†’ Reported: The student wanted to know where the registrar was.

### Reporting Commands, Requests, Advice, Orders

**Positive commands:**
Pattern: Subject + verb + object pronoun + infinitive with to + rest of sentence.
- Direct: "Be quiet!" â†’ Reported: The teacher told them to be quiet.
- Direct: She said, "Please turn off the light when you sleep." â†’ Reported: She asked me to turn off the light when I slept.

**Negative commands:**
Pattern: Subject + reporting verb + object pronoun + not + infinitive with to + rest of sentence.
- Direct: The sign says, "Do not smoke!" â†’ Reported: The sign tells us not to smoke.

## Chapter 5: Types of Writing

### 1. Expository Writing

The word expository contains the word "expose" â€” this type of writing exposes, or sets forth, facts. It is the most common writing genre. In an expository piece, a topic is introduced and laid out in logical order without reference to the author's personal opinions.

Expository writing can be found in: textbooks, journalism (except opinion and editorial articles), business writing, technical writing, essays, and instructions.

Key Points:
- Usually explains something in a process.
- Is often equipped with facts and figures.
- Is usually in a logical order and sequence.

### 2. Descriptive Writing

The aim of descriptive writing is to help the reader visualize, in detail, a character, event, place, or all of these things at once. The author might describe the scene in terms of all five senses.

Descriptive writing can be found in: fiction, poetry, advertising, and journal/diary writing.

Key Points:
- It is often poetic in nature.
- It describes places, people, events, situations, or locations in a highly-detailed manner.
- The author visualizes what he or she sees, hears, tastes, smells, and feels.

### 3. Persuasive Writing

The aim of persuasive writing, or argumentation, is to influence the reader to assume the author's point of view. The author will express personal opinions and arm him- or herself with evidence so that the reader will agree.

Persuasive writing can be found in: advertising, opinion and editorial pieces, reviews, job applications, cover letters, letters of recommendation, and letters of complaint.

Key Points:
- Equipped with reasons, arguments, and justifications.
- The author takes a stand and asks you to agree with his or her point of view.
- Often asks for readers to do something about the situation (a call-to-action).

### 4. Narrative Writing

The purpose of narrative writing is to tell a story, whether real or imaginary. Pieces in a narrative style will have characters, and through the narrative, the reader learns what happens to them. Narrative writing can also include dialogue.

Narrative writing can be found in: all types of fiction (novels, short stories, novellas), poetry, biographies, human interest stories, and anecdotes.

Key Points:
- A person tells a story or event.
- Has characters and dialogue.
- Has definite and logical beginnings, intervals, and endings.
- Often has situations like actions, motivational events, and disputes or conflicts with their eventual solutions.

## Chapter 6: Conditionals

Conditional tenses are used to speculate about what could happen, what might have happened, and what we wish would happen. In English, most conditional sentences contain the word "if." These sentences are made up of an if clause and a main clause. "Unless" can often replace "if" in negative conditional sentences.

| Conditional Type | Usage | If Clause | Main Clause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | General truths | Simple present | Simple present |
| Type 1 | A possible condition and its probable result | Simple present | Simple future |
| Type 2 | A hypothetical condition and its probable result | Simple past | Present conditional or present continuous |
| Type 3 | An unreal past condition and its probable result in the past | Past perfect | Perfect conditional |
| Mixed type | An unreal past condition and its probable result in the present | Past perfect | Present conditional |

### Zero Conditional

Used when the time is now or always and the situation is real and possible. Often used to refer to general truths. The word "if" can usually be replaced by "when" without changing the meaning.

- If + simple present â†’ simple present
- If you heat ice, it melts.
- If it rains, the grass gets wet.

### Type 1 Conditional

Used to refer to the present or future where the situation is real. Refers to a possible condition and its probable result.

- If + simple present â†’ simple future
- If you don't hurry, you will miss the train.
- If it rains today, you will get wet.

### Type 2 Conditional

Used to refer to a time that is now or any time, and a situation that is unreal. Not based on fact. Refers to a hypothetical condition and its probable result.

- If + simple past â†’ present conditional or present continuous conditional
- If you went to bed earlier, you would not be so tired.
- If it rained, you would get wet.
- If I spoke Italian, I would be working in Italy.

### Type 3 Conditional

Used to refer to a time that is in the past, and a situation that is contrary to reality. Refers to an unreal past condition and its probable past result.

- If + past perfect â†’ perfect conditional or perfect continuous conditional
- If you had studied harder, you would have passed the exam.
- If it had rained, you would have gotten wet.
- If I had accepted that promotion, I would have been working in Milan.

### Mixed Type Conditional

Used to refer to a time that is in the past, and a situation that is ongoing into the present.

- If + past perfect â†’ present conditional or perfect conditional
- If I had worked harder at school, I would have a better job now.
- If we had looked at the map, we wouldn't be lost.
