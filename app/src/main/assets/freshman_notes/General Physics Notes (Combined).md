## Chapter 1: Introduction to Physics

# Chapter 1

**1. Definition of Physics?**

Physics is a Greek word meaning nature. In a very broad sense, physics is the study of nature and natural phenomena, so in essence, you study physics to learn about the natural world. The goal of physics is for us to use observations and measurements to find fundamental laws and principles that explain how the world works. This will enable us to understand, explain, and make predictions about events in the universe. It is the most basic of natural science. It also strives to create mathematical models that describe the physical world.

**Importance of Learning Physics**

We are surrounded by physics in our everyday lives. Physics is the most fundamental of the natural sciences. It has wide applications in interdisciplinary science. It explores the entire universe, from the smallest subatomic particles to the largest galaxies.

**Physics and Technology**

Technology is the practical application of scientific discoveries. Technology is almost always the result of the physical sciences. The development of science and technology leads to scientific literacy, which is a basic understanding of science and technology necessary for informed decision-making in today's world. Examples: computers, cell phones, iPad, radio, television, and DVD. Energy source examples: Nuclear power plants, solar power system, wind power generator, hydro-electric power, and biogas plant.

**2. Units and Dimension**

A physical quantity is a physical property of a system that can be quantified by measurement. In order to determine a physical quantity, we must define:

1. A standard for the dimension we wish to measure (a standard for comparison), and
2. A procedure (experiment) for comparing the unknown quantity to the standard. This ratio gives us the number of units that measure the quantity.

To make measurements more useful, they should have certain characteristics. An ideal set of units should be:

1. Well-defined (no ambiguity as to what is being measured)
2. Of a convenient size (not too large or too small)
3. Reproducible anywhere in the world
4. Accurate

When a physical quantity is expressed in terms of a certain unit, the numerical value may vary depending on the units used. For example, the length of a table can be expressed as 100 cm or 1 m. The numerical value and the unit are inversely proportional to each other.

**Physical Quantities**

The value of a physical quantity is expressed as the product of a numerical value and a unit:
Value of quantity = Number Ã— Unit

**Significant Figures**

Significant figures in a measurement include:
- All the certain digits
- The first uncertain digit

- All non-zero digits are significant

**3. Coordinate System and Vectors**

Coordinate System
A coordinate system is a reference framework for measuring the numerical values of the location of points in space. It consists of:
- A fixed reference point (origin O)
- A set of axes with labels and directions
- A system for labeling the position of points in space numerically

In 1-dimensional motion, we use a single axis called an x-axis. In 2-dimensional motion, we use two axes: x and y (in a plane). In 3-dimensional motion, we use three axes: x, y, and z.

**Vectors and Scalars**

Physical quantities are divided into two categories:

- Scalar quantities: Have magnitude only (e.g., time, temperature, volume)
- Vector quantities: Have both magnitude and direction (e.g., displacement, velocity, force)

The magnitude of a vector V is written as |V| or V. We represent a vector by drawing an arrow whose length represents the magnitude of the quantity and whose direction represents the direction of the quantity.

**Unit Vector**

A unit vector is a vector that has a magnitude of 1 and has no units. It has no effect on the magnitude of the vector; its purpose is to point in a given direction.

**Addition and Subtraction of Vectors**

The graphical method of adding vectors is based on the head-to-tail method (also called parallelogram method):
1. Draw vector A
2. Then draw vector B starting from the head of A
3. The resultant R = A + B is the vector drawn from the tail of A to the head of B
4. This is called the triangle method of vector addition

Example: A student walks 3.0 km east and then 4.0 km north. The displacement is the straight line from the starting point to the ending point.

We can also find the resultant by resolving each vector into its rectangular components (using a coordinate system) and then summing the components.
