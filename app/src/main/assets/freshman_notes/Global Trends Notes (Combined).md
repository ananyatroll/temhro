## Chapter 1: Understanding International Relations (IR)

### 1.1. Defining IR

IR can be defined as a state of affairs/condition and as a field of study. As state of affairs:

The concept International contains two terms Inter and Nation.

Inter refers to relations between two or more actors and nation implies to a state or country.

Accordingly, for Goldstine, IR is defined as "relationships among the world's states or national governments".

However, others scholars defined IR broadly as a totality of relation between/among state and none-state actors, as well as, all types of movements (people, resource, idea/knowledge) that cross state boundaries to include international rivers.

So, IR is concerned with "all exchanges, transactions/dealings, interactions, flows of resource/information, and actions of every kind... among separate societies of the world" as states represent societies.

As a discipline/field of study,

IR is a branch of political science that study foreign affairs and relations among sovereign states within the international system: including the roles of non-state actors such as Inter-Governmental Organizations (IGOs), Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs), Multinational Corporations (MNCs), Churches, political parties, big cities, Individuals, Ethnic groups etc.

The relations between/among governmental and non-governmental actors are on political, economic, cultural, technological, environmental.

Particular areas of studies within the field of IR include diplomacy and diplomatic history, international law, peace and security, climate change, international organizations, international finance and economics, and communications among others.

Traditionally, IR studies have been giving attention to inter-state relationships such as war, alliance, diplomacy and trade among nations alone.

However, contemporary IR, deals with multifaceted interactions among governmental and nongovernmental actors on issues ranging from international security-to-global poverty reduction and the environment-to- technology transfer. E.g. The role of UN agencies like UNDP

Moreover, modern IR is also characterized by a intense multiplicity of actors other than States such as MNCs, IGOS, International Civil Society Movements (INGOS) and others.

Generally, IR includes a wider range of interactions among states and non-state actors that include:

- Political Relation- involves peaceful as well as non-peaceful relation among states. E.g. Conflict/war, diplomatic relation/cooperation etc.
- Economic Relation- deals with such relations as trade, investment, mutual-development, foreign aid, loan, common market, currency exchange, tariff or other issues of cooperation, competition and integration.
- Cultural Relation- interaction between or among states like in cultural exchanges such as music and art, sports, linguistic and religious issues, heritage protection.
- Social Relation- includes educational exchanges like scholarships, tourist flows and visits, health matters, immigration and refugee affairs.

Participation in international relations is inescapable, given that no state in the world is self-sufficient/self-reliant/ (in economic, military, technology wise).

Some states are endowed with abundant resources, some are not.

Some states may have the potential to growth but may not tap available resources effectively due to lack of appropriate technology/expertize. E.g. Most African States.

Whether states are big or small, rich or poor, powerful or weak, the existence and development of one state is highly dependent on the supply of resources or technology from others.

Hence, entering into relations with other countries is inevitable or unavoidable.

Generally, as a field of study, IR is:

1. The study of all human interactions across national boundaries and the factors that affect those interactions.
2. IR is the study of who gets what, when and how at the international stage.

To do so, the foundational questions of IR include:

- How can human nature be characterized and affect FPs?
- What is the relationship between the individual and society?
- What are the characteristics and role of the state and non-state actors in shaping the nature of global order?
- How is the international system organized and affect states' behaviour?

### 1.2. Historical Development of IR

Writing about international politics by historians, political philosophers, international lawyers has been there for many centuries.

Yet, IR as a discipline/filed of study is young that occurred at the end of WWI in 1919 and was born from the ash of war.

As a result, the original goal of the study of IR was to advance new world freed from the threat of war

The tragedy/disaster of WWI encouraged early IR scholars to focus on finding ways to build a more peaceful world.

As war gave birth to school of IR, the creation of global peace was its first mission.

IR scholars' preoccupation was dealing with international conflicts and secure peace and cooperation at international levels.

The Scholars base their inquiry on WWI by answering questions:

- what was the main cause of WWI?
- what was the nature of the old order?
- what were the lesson we learn from WWI?
- how could such a war be prevented?
- on what basis could a new international order created?
- how could IGOS such as League of Nations guarantee global peace and security?

Finally, IR Scholars conclude that 'international anarchy' (lacks of any supreme global authority that can resolve disputes, enforce law, or order the system of international politics) as cause of WWI and partly misunderstandings, miscalculations and irresponsibility on the part of respective state politicians.

### 1.3. Nature of IR

International Relation is an interdisciplinary discipline: It borrows knowledge and theoretical ideas from fields such as economics, history, law, philosophy, geography, sociology, anthropology, psychology, as well as, from cultural, peace, and federal studies.

### 1.4. Scope of IR

Traditionally, IR study focused on security mainly war and peace studies, mainly in the 1950s and 1960s.

But, throughout time IR expanded its scope into diversified issues.

The interdisciplinary peace studies programs and feminist scholarship have sought to broaden concepts of "security" to political, economic, military, socio-cultural, environmental issues:

Particularly IR studies include: International Political Economy, International environmental management, global telecommunication, human welfare, global warming, gender issues, ethnicity, identity politics and religious fundamentalism among others.

### 1.5. Actors of IR

Actors are individual/group who pursue certain goal through either cooperation/peaceful or conflict/war at the international stage.

Traditionally, state was the only actor in IR or inter-state relations was IR's main concern.

Contemporarily, the international context is changed and multiple actors are involved in international relations.

Simply, today, State is not the sole actor although act as primary actor.

Thus, there are two major actors in IR.

**State actors:** states are primary actors in IR.

States are sovereign actors (based on the principle of sate sovereignty under international law) with their governments that represent the people at the international stage.

States are the highest/supreme institutions both at the domestic and international level.

In this regard, there are two types of state sovereignty: internal and external.

Modern state (emerged after the 1648 Wetifalian Treaty that established European States) is unique from the traditional political systems (empires) as it exercise sovereign power over its territory and people as different from non-state actors.

Domestically, government decisions and actions are supreme over other institutions such as religious, family (citizen) or any kind of human association as they all obey the rules and commands of the state.

Likewise, sovereignty infers to state's external autonomy in its international relations. There is no any power that can intervene in internal affairs of the state.

Hence, in principle a state's government answers to no higher authority; it exercises both internal and external sovereignty over its territory—to make and enforce laws, to formulate and implement domestic and foreign policy, to collect taxes, and so forth.

**Non-state actors:** actors that cross national boundaries and frequently put influence and work with state over policy outcomes

Non-state actors include: ethnic groups, individuals, political parties, INGOs, IGOs, MNCs, churches and terrorist groups/organizations.

All in one way or another, influence our everyday experience/life, the conduct of interstate interaction and the global politics.

### 1.6. Level of Analysis

IRs is exposed to complex theories and explanations due to its interdisciplinary nature and the involvement of multiple actors.

So, to ease the complexity, IR theorists opted to use level of analysis.

The concept of "level of analysis" refers to the extent or scale at which a particular subject or phenomenon is studied.

It is related to explaining cause and effect of phenomenon at various levels to understand highly complex problems within world politics

In this regard, Kenneth Waltz in his book Man, the State and War: A Theoretical Analysis (1959) identified three level of analysis.

**Individual Level of Analysis:**

International events are the results of intended or unintended decisions/actions made by individuals. The focus study are individuals.

Here, IR scholars argue the behaviour, motivations, nature of human being, choice, ability, unique personality, biography, aim, belief and orientation of the individual are considered to have national, regional, and global implication.

They argue that certain choices and decisions made by different people at different times are the things that we need to be looking at in explaining or studying international relations/politics.

This level analysis might ask various questions like: Are there special or extraordinary political personalities for good or bad impact?

e.g.

1. Explain WWII by examining the role of Adolf Hitler,
2. Evaluate US president Gorge Bush and US invasion of Iraq,
3. Examine the end of cold war by looking at the role of Gorbachev.

**State Level of Analysis:**

It focuses on state foreign policy behaviour and orientations.

Exist between the individual and the system levels of analysis.

Consider states as actors in their own sovereign rights.

Unlike system-level of analysts, who believe that the international system pressures states to behave in certain ways, state-level analysts contend that states are relatively free to decide what policies to follow.

States are defined political entities that have certain preferences or national interests to be realized via their foreign policies.

Accordingly, state level of analysts look at State's policies, actions and decisions to answer analytical questions like:

- How states interact with each other to deal with joint interests as good partners.
- How states cooperate in the framework of international organizations like UN, EU, AU etc.; and
- How to look at states as competitors and antagonists considering: their geographic position, historical ties and experiences, economic wellbeing/capacity, and foreign policy orientation.

David Singer claims that:

The Key indicators would be the policies proposed and actions decided by governments, statements of top-level politicians but also the role of diplomats and their adjoining bureaucratic structures.

**System Level of Analysis:**

This level of analysis involves a top-down or general-specific approach to the study of world politics or IR.

Events or developments in one corner of the world would have spillover effect on the other corner of the world.

This level of enquiry examines state behavior by looking at the international system.

Under this level of analysis, nature of the international system is the cause and state behavior is the effect/consequence.

Characteristics of the international system cause states to behave the way they do.

The major weakness of this level of analysis is the lack of detail/depth in favour of studying the whole.

For system level analysts, the variables that helps to explain the occurrence of international events include:

Characteristics of the international system: International system has horizontal authority structure (with sovereign equality of states) and is anarchical.

By anarchical means:

- It is based the idea that there is no higher authority that exercises power over a sovereign states.
- It has no overarching authority to make rules, settle disputes, enforce international law, and provide protection.

Nature and influence of actors: what international organizations operate in the system and what impacts do they have on the course of international relations.

Roles and number of system poles: the number of major poles (power configurations) in the system is significant.

Political scientists believe that the pattern of interaction varies according to the number of poles that a system has.

There are three patterns/configurations:

- **Unipolar** - a system dominated by one super power/empire.
  - It is assumed that the hegemonic actor prevents or resolves conflicts by serving as "the Police Agent of the World."
  - Unipolar system is similar with an inter-state structure governed by a government because one state tries to assume a world government while other states become dependent and less influential.
  - Power structure is hierarchical in that power is concentrated in the hands of one powerful nation/empire.
  - No alliances exist because the hierarchy is ruled by one center of power. In history, we have experienced like the Romans and the Ottoman-Turks which assumed world powers at various epochs.
  - It is also argued after the Gulf War in 1991 and disintegration of USSR many states, including the United States' closest allies and developing states, concerned that the international system had become unipolar. E.g. USA

- **Bipolar** - A system dominated by two contending super powers which in turn dominate other states and the international system at large.
  - There are two coalitions/alliances formed and headed by the two super powers.
  - In fact, there are ideological ties within the bloc. Alliances are hierarchically organized with the two powers as the leaders of their respective satellite member states.
  - There is conflict between the two coalitions/blocs and especially proxy war between the bloc leaders.
  - In our contemporary world, the Cold War (1947-1991) period can be an example.

- **Multipolar** - power concentrated in more than two states.
  - In the post World War I, five states, Great Britain, Russia, Prussia, France, and Austria-Hungary, comprised a multipolar system that had evolved from the balance of power after the Napoleonic wars.

Economic patterns: influenced by economic interdependence, dependency, natural resource location and use, and the maldistribution of development (the North-South Dichotomy).

### 1.7. Theories of International Relations

Theories are viewpoints that can serve as lenses to study a phenomenon.

The study of IRs takes a wide range of theories concerned on issues related to:

- the regulation/rule of interstate relations, and
- the maintenance of peace and order in the international system

**1. Idealism/Liberalism:**

Arose after WWI in response to the failure of states to limit/stop war in their international relations.

Its intellectual tradition traced back to the European enlightenment period of the 17th century, particularly from the work of John Locke.

It focuses on understanding causes of war and its remedies questing for global governance i.e. League of Nations.

Major assumptions Include:

- Human nature is essentially good/altruistic-people are therefore capable of mutual aid and collaboration.
- There is a greater potential for cooperation among states even if the international system is anarchic.
- Therefore, for liberalists, States are sovereign, but not autonomous to mean state sovereignty has certain limits (sovereignty can be checked in some occasions).

Liberalists are more optimistic about the prospect for international peace and permanent cessation of war is attainable goal.

IGOs can reduce the danger of war-IGOs like League of Nations are important actors for collective action.

Liberalism sees international law and morality as key source of order in the international system-States obey that law and morality ensures global order.

Likewise, INGOs represent different interests and can facilitate collective actions.

NB: For Immanuel Kant, democracy within states contribute to the establishment of peace among states cause democracies rarely go to war.

The prospect for the elimination of war lay with a preference for democracy over aristocracy/dictatorship, free trade over autarky (economic self-sufficiency) and collective security over the balance of power system.

Balance of power is IR concept that suggests states may secure their survival by preventing any one state from gaining enough military power to dominate all through defensive coalition.

**2. Realism:**

Emerged as dominant IR theory in the late 1930s and early 1940s in response to idealism/liberalism.

But, this school of thought characterize international politics throughout the 18th, 19th and 20th Centuries.

Niccolo Machiavelli, Thomas Hobbes, and J.J. Rousseau are the most prominent realist thinkers.

Machiavelli starts his argument from a deeply pessimistic/negative view of human nature i.e. human being are not good and cooperative.

According to Machiavelli, power and security are the major concern, for efficient and strong rulers.

E.H. Carr and Hans Morgenthau- are modern realist thinkers. Particularly, Morgenthau is considered to be the "father of realism".

Realists criticize idealists' argument that it was possible to build international political system that can remove/avoid conflict/competition.

Major assumptions:

- Realists view human nature as flawed, egoistic and power-seeking.
- View state as self-centric, autonomous, power-seeking and rational actor.
- Realists are Skeptical about the existence of universal moral principle-State survival requires leaders to distance themselves from traditional notions of morality. There is no morality in politics.
- Realists view international system as anarchic- meant international politics takes place in an arena that has no overarching central authority above the collection of sovereign states (pessimism about Global governance).

Cooperation in an anarchic world is difficult due to the concern of relative gains (rather than absolute or mutual gain by states).

The nature of the competition is viewed in zero-sum terms (whatever gained by one side is lost by the other); in other words, more for one actor means less for another.

Hence, States are in a permanent state of insecurity.

Moreover, realists are skeptical about IGOs, international law, and INGOs, though they do not completely discount their place/role.

State power is the key and the only variable of interests and law can be enforced through state power.

Balance of power is the predominant feature of the international system.

States can pursue a policy of balance of power in two ways: by increasing their own power engaging in an armaments race or acquisition of territory; or by adding to their own power from other states, as when embarking upon a policy of alliances.

**3. Marxism:**

A fundamental critique to liberalism comes from the 19th century German philosopher and economist, Karl Marx.

Marx takes a zero-sum argument (win-loose) and applies it to relations of classes (the capitalist and working class) instead of states.

He saw the economy as a site of human exploitation and class inequality.

It basis on the theory of class contradiction: the capitalist class as exploiter and the working class as exploited.

The role of the state is doing the bidding of corporations/companies.

Marxists believe international cooperation among the working class will eventually bring just and fair international system.

There is no anarchy, rather hierarchy in the international system.

Economic power is the most crucial power to dominate others in which the capitalist class of states is the driving force.

Marxists are Skeptical about IGOs, INGOs and international law.

INGOs are largely based in the North and are dominated by members of the same elite that run the state and international organizations.

They see INGOs as falling under the pressures of the capitalist economic system and as captive to their dominant interests.

Regarding, IGOs-international law is biased against the interests of socialist states, the weak and the unrepresented.

All in all, capitalism has created two sharp and polarized economic classes, i.e. the proletariat and bourgeoisie.

The theory holds that the powerful classes oppress and exploit the less powerful by denying them their fair share of the surplus they create.

Marx saw capitalism and the market as creating extremes of wealth for capitalists and poverty for workers.

Thus, class struggle, is one way of looking at the political relationships between richer and poorer people, and finally between richer and poorer world regions.

Major assumptions of Marxism:

- Human nature is motivated by self-interest, egoism and readiness to dominate others.
- Actors in IRs are the classes-Classes are more vital actors than society and states.

## Chapter 2: National Interest, Foreign Policy and Diplomacy

### 2.1. National Interest

National interest is the primary goal that a country pursues through its foreign policy at the global stage.

National interest refers to the objectives, values, visions, and goals that states aspire to achieve through diplomatic relation.

It holds the aspiration of a country to be realized through its policies and programs, as well as, emotions and debates surrounding them (regarding their importance and priority).

National interest is the reason of a state to justify/defend its action/reaction towards other states.

All states want to achieve national interest goals based on their capability or power that allows them to do.

According to Morgenthau, the minimum goal a state would like to achieve is survival.

National interest of a state encompasses:

- The preservation of physical identity: the maintenance of territorial integrity of a state,
- The preservation of political identity and economic prosperity: the preservation of existing politico-economic system of a state-Promoting the economic well-being and competitiveness of a country through trade, investment, and resource access.
- The preservation of cultural identity: the preservation of ethnic, religious, linguistic and historical norms of the people of a state.
- Global influence and prestige: Enhancing a nation's international standing, leverage, and ability to shape world affairs.

---

### 2.2. Understanding Foreign Policy and FP Behaviour

**Foreign Policy Defined**

Foreign policy refers to the strategies, principles, and actions that a country takes in its relations with states, international organizations, and other global actors on the world stage. Simply, FP is an external (outward) oriented policy.

It refers to the set of objectives (national interest), orientation/position and the instruments that a state adopts to guide its relations with the external world

Foreign Policy refers to system of activities by states for changing the behavior of other states and for adjusting their own activities/behavior to the international environment.

**Foreign policy objectives**

National interest is the foreign policy objective of states.

FP objectives include long, middle and short range determined based on the combination of three criteria:

- the value placed on the objectives
- the time element placed on its achievements
- the kind of demands the objective impose on others

Short range objectives:

- also called core/primary values & interests
- basic principles of foreign policy
- everybody accept it without question
- includes the self-preservation of political & economic systems, the people & territorial integrity of a state

Middle range objectives:

- less important & less immediate
- economic development & social welfare
- include fulfilling material needs, economic needs, prestige of the nation, scientific & technological advancement.

Long range objectives:

- least immediate to policy makers
- includes -future plans, thoughts & visions concerning the ultimate political & ideological organization of the international system, & rules governing relations in that system.
- Maintain global influence and power projection capabilities
- Promote democracy, human rights, and a rules-based international order.
- Counter the growing influence of USA, China and Russia
- Ensure access to global trade routes and markets
- Prevent the proliferation of weapons of mass destruction

**Foreign policy behaviour: Patterns and Trends**

Foreign policy behaviour refers to the decision/action/measure that states take towards each other.

The actions are not ends in themselves, but are connected to FP objectives as means to an end. E.g. Peacefully/Diplomacy or Force/War.

Here, it is difficult to just categorize states as peace loving & war-like.

Arnold Wolfers a well known IR scholar suggested that all foreign policy behaviour ultimately spots down to three possible patterns:

- **Self-preservation:** maintaining the status quo (the existing state of affairs and power configuration (USA needed to continue as super power following IIWW in shaping and maintaining the workings of the UN, WB, IMF, and WTO).
- **Self-extension:** revising the status quo in one's own favour (newly emerging powers like China, India, Brazil, and Germany competing to restructure the international institutions and different regimes)
- **Self-Abnegation:** revising the status quo in other's favour (LDCs)-States that are weak and very much dependent on foreign aid are profoundly caught with many problems in order to pursue an autonomous policy.

---

### Foreign Policy Dimensions/Orientations

The Foreign policy behaviour analysis can be done through a number of dimensions considering changes over time and different style of leaderships and circumstances. The orientation identified in terms of: Alignment, scope & modus operandi.

**1. Alignment:** whether leaders choose to ally/collaborate with others or remain neutral.

Alignment is categorised: alliance, neutrality & nonalignment

- **Alliances** - formal agreements/treaties among independent states to achieve mutual military, economic and other interests. E.g. WTO, NATO, OPEC, UN, EU, AU, OAS etc.
- **Neutrality** - is a stance of formal non-partisanship in world affairs to avoid some of the problems associated with alliances, like generating potential enemies & counter alliances. International law allows a country to remain officially neutral during a period of war between two or more states or from any alliances. E.g. Switzerland, Costa Rica, Panama etc.
- **Non-alignment** - has been the foreign policy pattern/orientation of most developing states during the Cold War era (the USA-USSR dichotomy) manifested in disregarding both major power bloc-the West and East calling for the South-south cooperation. It was founded in 1961 by: Yugoslavia, India, Indonesia, Egypt, and Ghana.

**2. Scope:** the scope of the country's activities and interests

- some have extensive, far-reaching global contact
- others have limited activities or global reach

In this regard, there are three patterns: global terms, regional terms & isolationism.

- **Global terms** - Defining National Interest in global terms & having extensive IRs in nearly every region of the world. E.g. USA and more recently China.
- **Regional Terms** - Most countries in the world are essentially regional actors: Defining one's FP to interacting primarily with neighboring states in the same geographical area. E.g. AU, OAS, ASEAN, EU, BRICS etc.
- **Isolationism** - this policy prohibited foreign contact with most outside countries. In recent years, there has been a trend of some countries pursuing more isolationist policies. Countries that tend to pursue isolationist foreign policies generally believe that they are self-sufficient. Isolationism opposes all commitments to foreign countries including treaties and trade agreements. It is less viable foreign policy orientation in this interdependent and highly globalized world. E.g. Albania, Burma, Cambodia, North Korea etc.

**3. Modus operandi/Method of operation:** a Latin phrase that refers to the particular way in which states or organization carries out an activity or achieves a goal.

Under this FP dimension-States employ unilateral, bilateral and multilateral means to solve problems.

---

### Instrument of Foreign Policy

Foreign policy involves instruments/tactics to realize the FP goals/objectives of states.

Each FP instrument is used to affect the behaviors of other states, and has an element of power.

Instruments of FP includes:

**1. Diplomacy:** it exist as long as civilization exist

Diplomacy can be defined as a process of interaction between diplomats, usually representing states-who engage in private and public dialogue to pursue their objectives in peaceful manner.

It is a process that states employ to pursue their objectives in a peaceful manner.

To this end, diplomacy involves the use of negotiations, signing treaties, summits (leader to leader communication), and other diplomatic channels to build good relationships and influence others behavior.

Diplomacy includes or has the following typologies:

- bilateral/ambassadorial diplomacy,
- multilateral diplomacy,
- public diplomacy, and
- leader to leader or summit diplomacy-statesmen/leaders telephone each other.

Here, the basic principle of diplomacy is bargaining.

Bargaining refers to settling disputes/differences through an exchange of proposal to search for mutually acceptable solutions.

Diplomatic bargaining is used primarily to reach agreements, compromises, and settlements of disputes, where states' objectives are in conflict (indicate conflict of interest).

Modern Diplomacy is not limited to states, but also involve powerful non states actors such as international non-governmental organizations (INGOs) and international governmental organizations (IGOs).

Rules of effective diplomacy employed by diplomats:

- Be realistic- it is important to have goals that fit with your capability to achieve them.
- Be patient- being overly nervous can lead to concessions that are unwise and may convey weakness/vulnerability to an opponent.
- Be careful about the words to be said- diplomats should plan out and weighs words carefully.
- Leave a venue of retreat open- showing honor, saving face, or respect regardless of securing once interest.
- Seek common ground- it is important to maintain a degree of flexibility for reconciliation/compromise.
- Understand the other side intention-appreciate an opponent's perspective even if you do not agree with it.

In general, states make use of what are known to be_ carrot and stick approaches when they rely on such diplomatic tactics such as promise, rewards, threats, and punishment.

**2. Economic instruments:** these include trade policies, sanctions, aid, investment, and other economic devices that can be used to reward or punish other states.

As modern states are politically and economically/technologically interdependent, so do they rely up on each other for resources and commodities that enable them to develop and sustain viable economies.

Likewise, cognizant of mainly dependency situation, state may reward/punish states through the manipulation of economic policies.

Developed States use economic muscle/might to influence the behaviour of state and non-state actors through tariff, quota, boycott/embargo, loans, credits and currency manipulations, and foreign aid.

**3. Psychological Instrument:** states use this mechanism to positively/negatively influence the mind of one's own people and the other's to accept policies favourable to themselves.

This technique primarily includes propaganda – process of appealing to emotion rather than logical reason by creating fear, doubt, sympathy, anger, or a variety of other feelings to influence another state/s.

**4. Military Instrument:** use or threat of military force, such as troop deployments, naval presence, or military exercises to deter aggression or force a desired outcome.

It is used as a final option after diplomacy failed.

It includes deterrence, overtly threatening opponents, demonstration of violence and direct action/war may be employed to influence an opponent.

**5. International Organizations:** Participating in multilateral institutions like the United Nations, AU, EU, NATO, the WTO, etc. to coordinate collective action and influence global governance.

## Chapter 3: International Political Economy

The study of political economy has been dominated by a national and/or international level debate over the responsibilities of the state with regard to the economy.

### 3.1. National versus International Political Economy

Nationally, Political economy is the study of the intersection/connection between politics and economics in a country. E.g. Inequality vs instability, capitalist vs workers relation

At the domestic level, the debate poses the following pressing questions:

- Should the state be responsible for determining how the economy of a given country is to be organized and run?
- Should such responsibility be left to the market which is populated by self-serving individuals as private agents?
- Should housing, medical care, education, welfare be provided by private citizens or by the state?

Internationally, political economy examines the complex interactions between politics and economics at the global level.

IPE is concerned with the way in which political and economic factors interact at the global level.

Thus, IPE is a field of inquiry that studies the ever-changing relationship between governments, International Organizations, businesses (MNCs), and social forces across history and in different geographical areas worldwide.

To the end, IPE debate poses such pressing questions as:

- How should international trade be governed?
- How should international investment be governed?
- How should international finance be governed?
- What should/not be the role of international institutions like the IMF, WB and WTO in the governance of international finance, investment and trade?

The growing prominence of IPE as a field of study is a result of the continuing breakdown of disciplinary boundaries between economics and politics science.

The traditional/earlier IPE problematique (multi-faceted problems) includes analysis of the political economy of international trade, international finance, north-south relations, multinational corporations, and hegemony/domination.

The new/recent IPE's subjects include international development, regional cooperation and integration, environmental problems, globalization and its consequences.

Generally, IPE analyzes how political factors, such as government policies, international institutions, and geopolitical dynamics, shape global economy and are being shaped by economic forces such as trade, finance, production, and development.

Some key aspects of IPE analysis/study include:

- The role of the state in the global economy: IPE examines how national governments use economic tools (e.g. trade policy, industrial policy) to advance their political and strategic interests, as well as how the global economy constrains and enables state action.
- The politics of international economic institutions: IPE analyzes the power dynamics and decision-making processes within global economic organizations like the IMF, World Bank, and WTO, and how they impact the international distribution of wealth and power.
- The relationship between domestic and international economics: IPE looks at how domestic political and economic structures shape a country's engagement with the global economy, and vice versa.
- The political impacts of economic globalization: IPE explores how increased cross-border flows of trade, investment, and finance have affected issues like employment, inequality, national sovereignty, and the environment.
- The politics of development and inequality: IPE examines the power dynamics and political interests involved in the global distribution of resources and the development trajectories of different regions and countries.
- The role of non-state actors: IPE analyzes the political influence of multinational corporations, civil society groups, and other non-governmental actors in shaping international economic outcomes.

### 3.2. Theoretical perspectives of IPE

Theories are lenses to study politico-economic phenomenon.

Theories of IPE are developed to answer the following questions:

- How does politics shape the decisions that societies/states make about how to use the resources that are available to them?
- What are the consequences of these decisions?

#### 3.2.1. Theories of IPE

**1. Mercantilism**

Mercantilism refers to an economic theory and practice that dominated European economic thought and policy during the 16th to 18th centuries.

It was based on the belief that a nation's wealth and power were determined by accumulation of precious metals, particularly gold and silver.

Mercantilist policies aimed to maximize a country's exports and minimize its imports, with the goal of achieving a favorable/positive balance of trade (a trade surplus, that occurs when a country's exports exceed its imports).

Governments applied various measures to achieve this goal, including imposing tariffs (on imported goods) and other trade barriers to protect domestic industries, subsidizing exports, and establishing colonies to secure access to raw materials (via Expropriation) to the local industries.

Mercantilists advocated self-serving states by favouring exports, so that wealth could be accumulated on the expense of others.

Basic tenets/doctrines of mercantilism include:

- The government should involve in international trade for the purpose of protecting the interest of the state.
- Mercantilists fear that unregulated economy leads to anarchy and exploitation as greedy individuals enrich themselves disregarding others (other social sects).
- International economy is competitive not cooperative. They reject the notion of comparative advantage (relative benefit as a result of efficiency of production of a state than others) due to their more pessimistic assessment on human nature and resultant behavior of governments.
- Self-sufficiency-they advocate governments holding what they have and trying to reduce relying on other states.
- Protectionism_ they advise governments to employ a variety of economic policies to insulate/protect their domestic industries from foreign competition. E.g. using tariff, quota, export subsidies etc.

Moreover, Mercantilism is categorized into two:

- **Classical mercantilism** - was based on 3 central propositions:
  1. National power and wealth are tightly connected.
  2. Trade provides countries one way to acquire wealth from abroad if it generates a positive balance of trade or trade surplus (strongly discourage import).
  3. Some economic activities are more valuable than others. e.g. Manufacturing activity should be promoted, whereas agriculture and other non-manufacturing activities should be discouraged

- **Modern mercantilism (flexible)** - applies 3 propositions to contemporary international economic policy as follows:
  1. Economic strength is a critical component of national power.
  2. Trade is to be valued for exports, but government should discourage imports, whenever possible.
  3. Some forms of economic activities are more valuable than others.

But, both argued that state should play a greater role in determining how society's resources are allocated.

**2. Liberalism**

Emerged in the 18th century.

Unlike Mercantilism, liberal theorists generally view a favorable balance of trade as a less important goal, as they believe that the overall benefits of free trade and global economic integration outweigh the potential advantages of running a trade surplus (considering; political, cultural, and social factors).

This theory challenged all the propositions of mercantilism:

For liberalists, the purpose of economic activity is to enrich individuals (citizens), not to enhance state/national power.

Countries do not enrich themselves by running trade surpluses.

Countries are not necessarily made wealthier by producing manufactured goods, but by producing products at a relatively low cost at home to secure comparative advantage.

The central propositions of liberalism include:

- **Free trade** - minimization of gov't involvement in international trade
- **Invisible hand** - society would be better off without government interference in the economy (encouraging free market economy).
- **Believe in Comparative advantage** - If a country/individual is relatively more efficient in the production of a good than another country/individual.
- **Trade brings peace** - economic interdependence among states brings international peace (economic cooperation and integration brings peace).

**3. Marxism/Critical Theory**

Critics against liberalism

Marxist and Critical theorists are often skeptical of the notion of favorable/positive balance of trade (trade surplus), as they see it as manifestation of the unequal power dynamics and exploitation inherent in the global capitalist system.

Marxists argue that trade surpluses are often achieved by capitalists at the expense of workers and consumers in the exporting countries.

Capital/wealth is concentrated in the hand of capitalists.

The capitalist class controls the state.

LDCs are exploited by the capitalist states, the best example is the colonial structure following the industrial revolution in Europe.

International economy does not provide benefit to all countries i.e. all gains accrue to the capitalist countries. i.e. Global economy is a zero-sum game (winner versus loser).

NB: In summary, the three perspectives have different response to the following two vital questions:

How politics shape the allocation of resource?

- Mercantilist: state guides resource allocations
- Liberalist: state play little role
- Marxist: decisions are made by the capitalist class

What are the consequences of resource allocation?

- Mercantilist: to enhance national/state power
- Liberalist: to improve the welfare of the people
- Marxist: to concentrate wealth in the hands of capitalists.

Other succeeding theories include:

**4. Hegemonic Stability Theory (HST):**

Is a hybrid theory containing elements of mercantilism, liberalism, and even Marxism. Yet, its closest association is with mercantilism.

The basic argument of HST is: the root cause of the economic troubles that bedeviled Europe and much of the world in the Great Depression of the 1920s and 1930s was the absence of a benevolent hegemon—that is, a dominant state willing and able to take responsibility for the smooth/fair operation of the International economic system as a whole.

**5. Structuralism:**

Is a variant of the Marxist perspective and starts from a practical analysis of the specific structural problems of the international liberal capitalist economic system whose main feature is centre-periphery dependency relationship between the Global North and the Global South which permanently resulted in an "unequal (trade and investment) exchange."

**6. Developmental State Approach:**

Emerged after realizing the failure of neo-liberal development paradigm (in the 1980's) in solving economic problems in developing countries.

The concept of developmental state is a variant of mercantilism and advocates for the strong role of the state in the process of structural transformation.

The term developmental state thus refers to economic policy that a state intervenes and guides the direction and pace of economic development. E.g. the EPRDF Government.

### 3.3. Core Issues, Governing Institutions and Governance of International Political Economy

**International Trade and the WTO**

The World Trade Organization (WTO) is an international organization which sets the rules for global trade.

This organization was set up in 1995 as the successor to the General Agreement on Trade and Tariffs (GATT) created after the Second World War.

It has about 150 members.

Decisions are supposed to be taken unanimously but the major economic powers such as the US, EU and Japan have managed to use the WTO to frame rules of trade to advance their own interests.

The developing countries often complain of non-transparent procedures and being pushed around by big powers.

**International Investment and the WB**

The World Bank was created immediately after the Second World War in 1945.

Its activities are focused on the developing countries.

The World Bank currently has 189 member countries as of 2023.

It works for human development (such as education, health), agriculture and rural development, environmental protection, infrastructure, and governance (such as anti-corruption, development of legal institutions).

It provides loans/credits (require payment with interest) and grants (do not require repayment) to the member-countries.

In this way, it exercises enormous influence on the economic policies of developing countries.

It is often criticized for setting the economic agenda of the poorer nations, attaching stringent/harsh conditions to its loans and forcing free market reforms.

Unlike in the case of Europe, the impact of the WB on the development of developing countries has been at best controversial and at worst negative.

**International Finance and the IMF**

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) oversees those financial institutions and regulations that act at the international level.

The IMF has 184 member countries, but they do not enjoy an equal say. The top ten countries have 55 per cent of the votes.

They are the G-8 members (the US, Japan, Germany, France, the UK, Italy, Canada and Russia), plus Saudi Arabia and China.

The US alone has 17.4 per cent voting rights.

The global financial system is divided into two inter-related systems: a monetary system and a credit system.

The international monetary system can be defined as the relationship between and among national currencies. E.g. US Dollar versus Birr.

The credit system, refers to the framework of rules, agreements, institutions, and practices that facilitate the transnational flow of financial capital for the purposes of investment and trade financing.

In both cases, IMF is criticized for serving the interest of the great powers to the disadvantage of the LDCs.

## Chapter 4: Globalization and Regionalism

### Globalization Defined

Globalization refers to the interconnectedness and interdependence of the world's economies, cultures, and populations, driven by advances in technology, communication, and transportation.

The advents of modern transportation and information communication technology (ICT) are the catalyst of globalization.

Hence, for David Harvey – globalization is the time-space compression manifested by shrink the world into a small village.

Globalisation is a process that removes all national barriers for the free movement of international capital, including human, material and resources as well as cultures assimilation that promote societal building across the globe.

It is the rise of global social relations (via ICT and print/electronic media), which link distant localities in such a way that local events are shaped by good or bad occurrences many miles away.

Generally, Globalization has the following key aspects:

- **Economic Globalization:** This refers to the increasing integration of national economies through the cross-border movement of goods, services, capital, and labor.
- **Technological Globalization:** Rapid technological progress, particularly in information and communication technologies, has enabled the flow of information, ideas, and cultural products across the globe speedily.
- **Cultural Globalization:** The increased interconnectedness of cultures worldwide has led to the exchange and mixing of cultural practices, values, and symbols. This has concerns about homogenization.
- **Political Globalization:** The rise of international organizations, treaties, and agreements has increased coordination and cooperation on global issues like the environment, security, and human rights.
- **Social Globalization:** Migration patterns, the spread of social movements, and the global nature of issues like education and public health have fostered greater social interconnectedness worldwide.

### Debates on Globalization

The debate revolves around the relationship between globalization and the role of nation state or issue of sovereignty by the three well known theoretical perspectives of globalization: hyper-globalism, skepticism, and transformationalism

Here, there are three key questions:

- is the nation state being undermined/challenged?
- has nation state retained its primacy/importance?
- is nation state becoming transformed by globalization in new ways?

**Hyper-globalism:**

Emerged in the late 20th and early 21st C. to describe an extraordinary level of global interconnectedness and integration.

According to this theory:

- The role of the nation state is diminished by the existence of international organizations such as the United Nations, the International Monetary fund (IMF), Multinational Corporations or by social movements (INGOs).
- The world is on its way to a form of global governance, rather than a system of governance by powerful nation states.
- So, the thinkers are optimists about global governance or the role of IGOs such as UN.

Nation-states become old-fashioned to regulate their economy and boundary.

Globalization brought denationalization (the diminishing role and influence of nation-states) and deterritorialization (social, cultural, and economic activities become less tied to specific geographic locations or territories).

Economic Globalization brings the decline of the state as IMF, World Bank and WTO have created a new economic order, which must be obeyed by nation states.

Simply, international organizations diminish the role of the nation state, and global governance by IGOs will become the last frontier.

National governments unable to control trans-boundary flow of goods, services, ideas, crimes, and security threats.

Nation states will eventually vanish/disappear, and will not transform any kind of structure in the future.

Globalization aims to create uniform way of life (universalization) by trivializing/undermining traditional culture of societies.

This theory is criticized for undermining the role of powerful states.

**Skepticism:**

Is opposed reactions to hyper-globalism

- Nation states are still active in shaping the nature of world politics
- Globalization is not a new process.
- The role of the nation state is still alive and its borders are effective.
- The organs of the United Nations (UN) are instruments of powerful nation states and are designed to achieve their political aims.
- The future of world politics will be related to national states and their operation.
- There are anti-globalist movements – a large number of people in the world are not comfortable with the idea of global governance. E.g. UN, IMF, WB...
- Powerful States are central actors and agents of globalization imposing their values and interests upon others.
- States play central role in shaping and regulating economic activities in the processes of globalisation or international political economy.
- Thus, this theory rejects the idea of global governance.
- It rejects the view of hyper-globalists as flawed, impractical, myth, and politically naÃ¯ve/immature.

What is happening in the name of globalization is internationalism (universalization of western value/culture), regionalism, and imposing neo-liberal policies created by the capitalist order. Hence, globalization brings nothing new.

**Transformationalism:**

It presents the middle ground between the hyper-globalists and skeptics by advocating concurrent role of states and globalization.

Transformationalists argue that the processes of hyper-globalism manifested by denationalization, and deterritorialization represent fundamental transformations in the structure and dynamics of the international order.

There are still nation states that exist in the context of world politics whose structures are different from before, yet the effect of globalization on nation states is undeniable.

Globalization is a real phenomenon and is affecting nation states

External forces (global governance issues under IGOS/INGOs) such as human rights, democracy, population policy, and factors such as the environment, education, labor, and immigration, all have an enormous role to play in reshaping the structures of nation states.

Whereas, the nation state still plays a role in world politics/economics.

Although international laws and the international organizations pressured national sovereignty; national institutions (states) are major players to put the international laws and other decisions and strategies into practice.

However, they still believe a new sovereign regime (global governance) is displacing traditional conception of state power as unqualified, indivisible and territorially exclusive power eligible to external interventions.

Thus, globalization reconstitute/reengineer the power, function, and authority of the state.

### Advantage and Disadvantage of Globalization

**Advantages:**

- Expansion of democratic culture, human right and the protection of historically minority and subaltern groups.
- Knowledge Transfer: Innovation in science, medicine, and technology and information communication which enabled the improvement of quality of life.
- Agricultural technological expansion, which could be helpful in alleviating poverty.
- Technological and social revolution that resulted in human security and safety.
- The free movement of good, service, people, ideas, expertise, knowledge and technology which has brought international interdependence enhancing peace and mutual advantage.
- New sense of global society and the perspective of global citizenship.

**Disadvantages:**

- Western imperialism of ideas and beliefs eroding and inroad the sovereignty of non-Western countries.
- Global capital and international financial institutions like WB and IMF made free inroads/intrusions into countries of the south
- Cultural imperialism- assimilation of different way of life and cultural values.
- Risks, threats and vulnerabilities like global terrorism, religious fundamentalism, proliferation of Small Arms and Light Weapons (SALWs), arms and human trafficking.
- It has stimulated a simultaneous but opposite process, which involves integration to the world and differentiation to the local.

### Regionalism

Emerged in Western Europe in the late 1940s, Regionalism is a political ideology and movement that seeks to promote the interests of a particular region or geographic area.

Region can be defined as a limited number of states linked together by a geographical proximity and by a degree of mutual interdependence.

Regionalism refers to intensifying political and/or economic processes of integration among states in particular geographic regions.

It is an ideology focusing on the cooperation and integration among states within one or more regions.

Regionalism depends on the elements of identifiable geographical regions, geographical proximity, and a regional organization with a common sense of identity and purpose.

Regionalism can be categorized into two:

- **Old regionalism:** regional association featured by protection from effects of globalization, trade liberalization, and more affected by politics. It advocates protectionist policy (using trade barriers) within the region and economic/trade integration is limited to member states (intra-regional).
- **New/Open regionalism:** open and outward oriented regionalism, driven by market and less by politics, admits globalization changes the balance from state regulation to market competition so that various regions compete. It allows inter-regional trade and is more diverse in geographical coverage as building block of globalization.

### Major Theories of Regional Integration

Regional integration is a process where states enter into a regional agreement to enhance integration through regional institutions and rules with strong loyalty of membership.

Purpose: economic, political, military, social and environmental

Regional integration is the highest stage of regional cooperation.

It is based on sacrifices some of the ultimate decision-making power of the nation-state to a regional organization (Supranational organization).

**A. Functionalism:**

A gradual bottom-up approach to integration emerged during 1940s that believe war is the result of social and economic maladministration.

The real task of regional integration is the conquest of poverty, ignorance, and disease.

The existing system based on state sovereignty is inadequate and an obstacle to finding solutions to global problems.

Regional Integration is functional response by states to regional problems derived from interdependence.

Regional integration must start from low politics (economic and social interaction of the people).

Reject the political aspect of regional integration.

Technocrats (individuals with technical expertise and scientific evidence) should rule the integration process.

Supranational institutions are the main actors in the integration

This theory is neglected anticipated problems like conflicts, hard power decisions and political mobilization (as its weak point).

**B. Neo-functionalism:**

Popular in Europe in the 1950s and 1960s as successor of functionalism.

The theory bases on three main processes:

- The theory views interest groups, political parties, nation states and supranational institutions as actors in regional integration.
- Good will and common interests among states will ultimately leads greater integration
- The context in which integration take place is economic, social and technical and to a lesser extent political aspects.

**C. Intergovernmentalism:**

Regional integration can be best understood as a series of rational choices made by state leaders (top-down approach)

Integration takes place within the context of domestic politics

Integration is a logical consequences of intergovernmental negotiation

It downgrade the importance of supranational organization

Effectiveness of regional integration is determined by the role of the state or state leaders played.

States are the main actor in the integration process and the international system

The process of integration includes:

- national preference formation
- inter-state bargaining
- institutional choice

The integrating units must be political community rejecting involvement of the private sector/business community.

**D. Supra-nationalism:**

Supra-nationalism refers to the transfer of power and decision-making from the nation-state level to supranational or regional organization level. E.g. EU as model autonomous supranational organization

The supranational perception of European integration, has been derived from: Neo-functionalism.

Accordingly, there are three mechanisms through which the European integration progresses:

1. first, positive spillover effects-refers to the phenomenon where cooperation in one policy area leads to increased cooperation and integration in other related policy areas;
2. second, a transfer of duties/sovereignty from the national to the supranational political arena (supranational organization); and
3. third, a 'technocratic automaticity,' referring to an increasingly role of technological systems and expert knowledge in decision making and governance in promoting integration.
